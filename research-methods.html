▼ Unit 3: Methodology and Research Methods ▼

Unit 3 Learning Outcomes:

1. Understand the different research methods.

2. Know which data collection methods are related to each method of research.

3. Have some idea which of these would be suited to your area of research.

  • Collaborative Discussion 1- Summary Post
  • Activity 2: Research Aims
  • Tutor FeedBack

    • --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    This unit was about the big ideas, or philosophies, that guide research: positivism, interpretivism, and pragmatism. At first, I found these concepts difficult and confusing. But as I learned more, I began to see how they affect the way we do research, collect information, and understand results. Positivism is based on the idea that we can measure the world in an objective way. It uses numbers, facts, and data to find patterns. This type of thinking is common in science and works well with quantitative research. On the other hand, interpretivism is about understanding people’s thoughts, feelings, and experiences. It is more personal and uses qualitative methods like interviews or observations (Bryman, 2016). Personally, I feel more drawn to pragmatism. Pragmatism is more flexible. It says we can use both numbers and words—quantitative and qualitative methods—depending on what the research question needs. This approach makes sense to me because many problems in the real world are complex and need more than one way to study them. By the end of this unit, I was able to think about my own research beliefs. I now understand that choosing a research philosophy is not just a theory, it helps guide all the steps of research. This learning will help me design better research in the future.

2. Know which method would be suitable, if any, for your investigation.

3. Consider the type of data you would obtain.

  • Tutor FeedBack

    In Unit 4, we delved into qualitative methods of data collection. I was especially interested in case study research, which enables an in-depth examination of a specific subject within its real-life context (Stake, 1995). This method resonated with me because of its potential to explore rich, contextual narratives. I learned the importance of participant observation, especially in ethnographic studies. However, I realized it can present ethical dilemmas, particularly in covert research, which requires careful ethical consideration. Focus groups were another method I learned about. I had underestimated their complexity before, but now I understand that group dynamics can yield deeper insights when well-facilitated. However, they can be influenced by dominant voices or social desirability bias. This unit taught me to value depth and nuance in research, and that careful planning is necessary for qualitative data to be meaningful and credible.

2. Understand how pre- and post-testing can be used in research.

3. Design a questionnaire to obtain the responses you will need for your investigation.

  • Reflective Activity 2

    • --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Unit 5 examined two of the most common data collection tools—interviews and surveys. I discovered the strengths and limitations of both and developed skills in designing each method. I conducted a mock semi-structured interview and found that it required not just prepared questions but active listening and the ability to follow interesting leads. This flexibility is a major advantage of qualitative interviews, allowing rich, detailed data to emerge. Conversely, I learned that surveys offer breadth over depth. They can quickly collect data from a large number of participants but are limited by question structure and response rate. Creating balanced and unbiased survey questions was more challenging than I expected. This unit helped me realize that both methods are valid depending on the research aims. Choosing between them involves trade-offs between time, resources, and the type of data needed.

▼ Unit 6: Quantitative Methods ▼

Unit 6 Learning Outcomes:

1. Understand and apply descriptive statistics.

2. Identify the different levels of measurement.

3. Produce measures of location and spread.

    --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    This unit was a turning point for me. I had always struggled with numerical data, but Unit 6 made statistics feel approachable. We covered basic concepts like mean, median, mode, frequency distributions, and measures of spread. One key insight was understanding how standard deviation tells us about the variability of a dataset, which has implications for reliability. We explored correlation coefficients, which show relationships between variables but do not prove causation. We practiced using Excel and Jamovi for data analysis. Jamovi, in particular, made statistical tests easier to perform, and I found it more intuitive than SPSS. This practical experience boosted my confidence in handling numbers and interpreting quantitative results.

    Building on the previous unit, Unit 7 introduced me to inferential statistics. I learned about hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, and p-values. ▼ Unit 9: Validity and Generalisability in Research ▼

Unit 9 Learning Outcomes:

1. Understand how the concepts of validity, generalisability and reliability affect your investigation and the design of your research method.

2. Consider how to analyse and present the results you obtain from your investigation and how they will enable you to answer your research question.

  • Collaborative Discussion 2- Summary Post
  • Tutor FeedBack

    • --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    This unit emphasised evaluating the quality of research, which I now recognize as vital. We explored three core concepts: validity, reliability, and generalisability. Internal validity refers to whether the study truly measures what it claims to, while reliability ensures consistency across measurements. External validity, or generalisability, refers to how far findings apply to other contexts or populations. I realized that in qualitative research, concepts like credibility, transferability, and dependability serve similar functions. For example, keeping a reflexive journal during fieldwork can improve transparency and trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). This unit reminded me that even well-designed studies must report their limitations openly. Acknowledging these does not weaken research—it strengthens its integrity.
